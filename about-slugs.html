A Game About Slugs

note from the developer

What's the deal with snail shells, anyway?

I crafted this metaphor during the early days of my gender exploration. When I was a trans dude, I wanted to communicate to the world that my identity was as if I was a snail that didn't always have its shell. That it was simple. That it could easily be explained away and I wasn't as weird and complicated as I felt deep down.

Thus, A Game About Snails was born! Done as the final project for one of my college classes, it was a short visual novel about a snail who was born a slug. The game itself wasn’t much, but it functioned as the creative outlet I needed at the time.

The years went by and things changed. I met with some brutal junctions in life, and my gender transition didn't survive them. It felt like the logical next step was to revisit this body of work: I decided I wanted to make another game with this character.

For a while I wrestled with the idea and couldn't figure out what to do. I was in a slump because I needed to answer to this body of work as if I was answering to the years I spent trying to be something I wasn't.

The whole point of A Game About Snails was that Slime Tony was truly a snail, not just a slug with a shell, and that was their true self. They were happy and confident and nothing was wrong.

But when the detransition happened, the shell did come off. The entire argument collapsed.

The first drafts came out raw, angry, and ugly. It essentially became "I ruined my life, I'm damaged, I'm Slime Tony and everything is awful."

I went in circles. No matter what, the story wasn't coming out right. The angry version just kept sounding like everyone else's opinion of me, and the original game had been so completely, unabashedly mine that I couldn't follow it up with something that fake.

It took a while to figure out what the sequel actually needed to be.

Sorry, it was never about gastropods

Being a girl was like I'd been dealt the shittiest hand in the deck. Even when people said I had a choice, it was an illusion, because that "choice" was contingent on those stronger than me giving any weight to it.

So I switched teams! It totally ruled at first. I felt happy and confident and powerful. I was finally liberated from the social contract that came with the gender I was born as.

But it turned out that wasn’t quite it.

I didn’t realize it at first, but there’s a social contract that comes with being a guy too, it just looks different. It came with real advantages, many of which I still envy, but I found myself sacrificing parts of who I was just to be accepted as one.

This new identity became armor I couldn't take off. It became a shell I couldn't remove. It stopped me from being stepped on, but it also kept me from knowing myself directly.

Both roads lead to nowhere

Detransitioning sucked majorly. I wasn't happy anymore with being a man, and when I was convinced to try to be a woman again, it was even worse.

People kept offering me “correct” versions of myself. Each of them had no room for the other, they were mutually exclusive. It was a real problem for me.

One said: If you're really a man, you should be masculine enough. The other said: If you're truly accepting yourself, you should feminize correctly.

The conclusion that I had to come to to make this sequel is: screw both of you. I'm not just microwave nachos of what could have been a normal person.

I didn’t need to bury my old self, I just had to steal his shit. I can keep the confidence and the willingness to reinvent myself and whatever was in that old identity that was genuinely mine, and integrate it with what I’ve learned about myself now.

Those traits don't belong to a gender, they belong to me. I was at war with my gender because I believed the wrong one was why my life felt unlivable. That just isn't true. Whether I'm a boy or a girl or whatever, it doesn't need to mean anything more than that.

What it means to live

The shell got me through those years.

After a while, being inside it began to hurt.

Being ripped out of it hurt even worse.

But it doesn't need to be discarded. It has a place in my garden. I don't need to be at peace with anything that happened with it. The only thing I need to be is myself, even if that means relearning what that looks like along the way.

That's what makes A Game About Slugs so much more powerful than just a "correction" of the first game. I'm not disavowing the shell metaphor — I'm completing it. It's an exploration of what it actually meant all along. What it was always going to mean once I'd lived through enough of the experience to see the full picture. Which is much more honest and complete than the angry, damaged version I was trying to force at first.

That version wouldn't have been mine anyway, it would have been someone else's.

The anger I felt at my past self was actually grief over the fact that the protection I needed so badly came with a cost I couldn't have anticipated. But my past self wasn't wrong to reach for that shell. I was doing what I felt I needed to do to survive. That doesn't mean that my current self is wrong to take it off and feel the rain, either.

This body of work only became possible when I stopped needing it to be an apology or a confession or a cautionary tale, and let it be what it actually is: a meditation on the things we do to survive, what survival costs us, and why it's worth it anyway.

To those that found this and read this far, thank you, from the bottom of my heart. I hope you enjoyed.
