A Game About Slugs

note from the developer

What's the deal with snail shells, anyway?

I crafted this metaphor during the early days of my gender exploration. When I was female-to-male, I wanted to communicate to the world that my identity was as if I was a snail that didn't always have its shell. That it was simple. That it could easily be explained away and I wasn't as weird and complicated as I felt deep down.

Thus, A Game About Snails was born! Done as the final project for one of my college classes, it was a short visual novel about a snail who was born a slug. The game itself wasn’t much, but it functioned as the creative outlet I needed at the time.

Obviously years passed and things changed. I met with some brutal junctions in life and came out the other end of them deciding to undo my gender transition. It felt like the logical next step was to revisit this body of work: I decided I wanted to make another game with this character.

When I began to make this sequel I wanted to correct that old story, it had been so expressive for me back then but now felt wrong. The whole point of A Game About Snails was that Slime Tony was truly a snail, not just a slug with a shell, and that was her true self. She was happy and confident and nothing was wrong.

But when I chose to detransition, the shell did come off. The entire argument collapsed.

Less of a sequel, more of an answer

For a while I wrestled with the idea, and couldn't figure out what to do. I was in a slump because I needed to answer to this body of work as if I was answering to the years I spent trying to be something I wasn't.

The first drafts came out raw, angry, and ugly. I was grieving because I was told that was how I should be feeling. Few people have experience with someone in their lives transitioning, and even fewer experience someone undoing a gender transition.

It was messy.

Most people in my life immediately took pity on me because they felt that was the right thing to do. They felt sorry for me and came crying about how tragic it must be to live as an experiment gone wrong. Microwave nachos of what could have been a normal person.

Naturally, I internalized this rhetoric, and Slime Tony’s simple story about being yourself and loving others had gone sour.

It essentially became "I ruined my life, I'm damaged, I'm Slime Tony and everything is awful."

I went in circles. No matter what, the story wasn't coming out right. I couldn’t reconcile the slug to snail metaphor because I couldn’t reconcile myself.

In hindsight, there simply was no story to tell yet. I still needed time. I realized, over months, that I didn't pick the wrong metaphor — it's that the metaphor came true. It was accurate in ways I couldn't see then because I was still living inside it. I devised this snail shell metaphor years ago, without understanding the real depth, just as I didn’t understand the depth of myself.

After the shell came off, I thought I had everything I needed to understand it clearly, but I didn’t. I had to figure out what really had been going on in my head.

Sorry, it was never about gastropods

Being a girl was like I'd been dealt the shittiest hand in the deck. Even when I was safe, I was never free, because a man could take whatever he wanted if he wanted it. Because he had the power. Even when people said I had a choice it was an illusion because that "choice" was contingent on those stronger than me giving any weight to it.

That was my reality. That's the reality of the slug — the creature without a shell, without protection from within, who can be squashed at any moment.

And I hate that. I hate that so much. Having to subscribe to a lifetime of borrowed safety.

So I switched teams! It totally ruled at first. I felt happy and confident and powerful. I was finally liberated from the social contract that came with being a woman.

But it turned out that wasn’t really enough.

I didn’t realize it at first, but there’s a social contract that comes with being a guy too, it just looks different. It came with real advantages, many of which I still envy, but I found myself sacrificing parts of who I was just to be accepted as one.

This new identity became armor I couldn't take off. It became a shell I couldn't remove. It stopped me from being stepped on, but it also kept me from knowing myself directly.

My freedom wasn’t really as free as I thought. I just swapped one cage for the other.

Crossroads

When I detransitioned I got pulled in a couple different directions.

On one hand, I had people telling me I’d regret it. That my trans self was who I really was and that I shouldn’t reject him. On the other hand, I had people who were glad I came around and were ready to usher me into the feminine identity I had spent my whole life rejecting.

I kept getting offered “correct” versions of myself. Each of them had no room for the other, they were mutually exclusive, and it was a real problem for me.

One said: If you're really a man, you should be masculine enough. The other said: If you're really accepting your female sex, you should feminize correctly.

The conclusion that I had to come to to make this sequel is: screw both of you.

I didn’t need to bury my old self, I just had to steal his shit. I can keep the confidence and the willingness to reinvent myself and whatever was in that old identity that was genuinely mine, and integrate it with what I’ve learned about myself now.

My whole life I had been dismissing my womanhood on the basis of it being an inferior mode of existence, like the life I wanted to live wasn't attainable in that state.

That just isn't true. Whether I'm a boy or a girl or whatever, it doesn't need to mean anything more than that. The tradeoff is never worth the cost of yourself.

What it means to live

The shell got me through those years.

It protected me, constrained me, and taught me things. Yes, I'm marked by it. But you know what? I'm also actually okay. It doesn't need to be discarded. It has a place in my garden.

I owe everything to it. I wouldn't have achieved this level of understanding if it weren't for this journey of mine. I learned to be myself, and that's all I ever wanted.

That's what makes A Game About Slugs so much more powerful than just a "correction" of the first game. I'm not disavowing the shell metaphor — I'm completing it. It's an exploration of what it actually meant all along. What it was always going to mean once I'd lived through enough of the experience to see the full picture. Which is much more honest and complete than the angry, damaged version I was trying to force at first.

That version wouldn't have been mine anyway, it would have been someone else's.

The anger I felt at my past self was actually grief over the fact that the protection I needed so badly came with a cost I couldn't have anticipated. But my past self wasn't wrong to reach for that shell. She was doing what she felt she needed to do to survive. That doesn't mean that my current self is wrong to take it off and feel the rain, either.

This body of work only became possible when I stopped needing it to be an apology or a confession or a cautionary tale, and let it be what it actually is: a meditation on the things we do to survive, what survival costs us, and why it's worth it anyway.

To those that found this and read this far, thank you, from the bottom of my heart. I hope you enjoyed.
